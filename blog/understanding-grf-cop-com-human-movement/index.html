Understanding GRF, COP and COM in Human Movement

Three short abbreviations appear again and again in biomechanics: GRF, COP and COM.

Three short abbreviations appear again and again in biomechanics: GRF, COP and COM.

They sound technical, but each one describes something you can see in everyday movement. Together they explain how we stand, balance, walk and lift.

COM: Centre of mass

The centre of mass (COM) is the single point where the body's mass can be considered to be concentrated. Think of it as the balance point.

In a person standing upright, the COM sits roughly in front of the lower spine, near the level of the pelvis.

It is not fixed. It moves whenever body parts move. Raise your arms and it moves up.

Bend forward and it moves forward and may even sit outside the body. When someone holds a load, the combined COM of person and load shifts toward the load.

Balance depends on keeping the COM controlled over the base of support.

GRF: Ground reaction force

Whenever you push on the ground, the ground pushes back with an equal and opposite force. This is Newton's third law, and the push-back is the ground reaction force (GRF).

GRF is a vector, so it has direction. It is usually split into three components:

  • Vertical: up and down. This is the largest component in most activities.
  • Anterior-posterior: forward and backward, linked to braking and propulsion.
  • Medio-lateral: side to side, linked to stability and changing direction.

GRF is measured with force plates. A few examples:

  • In quiet standing, the vertical GRF equals body weight.
  • In walking, vertical GRF peaks at roughly 1.1 to 1.2 times body weight.
  • In running, it can reach about 2 to 3 times body weight or more.
  • In a jump landing, it can be several times body weight within milliseconds.

This is why landing and running technique matter so much for joint loading.

COP: Centre of pressure

The centre of pressure (COP) is the point on the ground where the total GRF is considered to act. Imagine the whole pressure pattern under your feet collapsed into one point.

  • In quiet standing, the COP stays under your feet and moves slightly as you sway.
  • In walking, the COP travels from the heel along the outer foot toward the big toe as the foot rolls through stance.

During balance tasks, the COP path shows how much you are working to stay upright.

How COM and COP work together

COM is where the body's mass is. COP is where the ground force acts. They are different things, but they are closely linked.

  • In stable standing, the COM stays above the base of support, and the COP shifts to keep it there.
  • If the COM moves toward the edge of the base of support, the COP moves in the same direction to try to stop it, then overshoots slightly to bring the COM back.
  • A large gap between COP and COM means the body is accelerating. During walking, the COM actually moves outside the base of support for part of each step, which is why walking is described as controlled falling.

For balance training, this relationship is the key idea. We train the body to keep the COM under control by moving the COP effectively.

Practical uses for trainers

  1. Balance work: narrowing the base of support or lifting a foot makes it harder to keep the COM over the COP.
  2. Squats and deadlifts: the combined COM of body and barbell should stay over the midfoot. Heel or toe drift signals balance problems.
  3. Gait coaching: watch how the foot rolls through. Early heel lift or collapsing arches show up in the COP path.
  4. Landing mechanics: teach soft, controlled landings to spread GRF over time.
  5. Load carrying: a heavy load held in front shifts the COM forward, so the body leans back to compensate.

Final thoughts

COM tells you where the mass is, GRF tells you how hard the ground is pushing back, and COP tells you where that push acts. Once you see these three together, balance and gait become much easier to read.
